# HOO, SHO and LRGB

Colour images of deep-sky objects are usually assembled from several monochrome exposures taken through different filters. The way these channels are mapped to the red, green and blue channels of the final image is called a palette. The most common schemes are LRGB for broadband targets and HOO and SHO for narrowband targets.

## LRGB

LRGB combines a luminance channel (L) with red, green and blue channels. The luminance filter passes the whole visible spectrum and collects most of the signal, so it carries the detail and the signal-to-noise ratio of the image. The red, green and blue filters provide the colour information and can be shorter or binned. LRGB suits targets that emit a continuous spectrum: galaxies, star clusters, reflection nebulae and dark nebulae. Its weakness is light pollution and moonlight, which are also broadband and reduce contrast. A one-shot colour camera records the RGB part in a single exposure through its Bayer matrix.

## Narrowband filters

Emission nebulae shine in a few spectral lines. The most important for imaging are hydrogen-alpha (Ha) at 656.3 nm, doubly ionized oxygen (OIII) at 495.9 and 500.7 nm, and singly ionized sulphur (SII) at 671.6 nm. Narrowband filters pass only a few nanometres around these lines. They block most light pollution and moonlight, which makes narrowband imaging possible from cities and on moonlit nights.

## HOO

HOO uses two filters: Ha is mapped to red, and OIII is mapped to both green and blue. The result resembles the natural colours of many emission nebulae, with red hydrogen regions and teal oxygen regions. HOO needs only two filters and is popular with dual-band filters on colour cameras, which pass Ha and OIII at the same time. It works well for planetary nebulae, supernova remnants and nebulae with strong oxygen emission.

## SHO

SHO, also called the Hubble palette, maps SII to red, Ha to green and OIII to blue. Because hydrogen usually dominates, raw SHO images look green; processing shifts the hues so that hydrogen appears golden and oxygen blue. SHO separates regions of different ionization and shows structure that HOO hides, at the cost of a third filter and long exposures for the often faint SII signal. It is a false-colour palette: the colours encode chemistry rather than what the eye would see.

## Choosing a palette

Broadband targets such as galaxies call for LRGB or RGB. Emission nebulae call for narrowband: HOO when two filters are available or oxygen is strong, SHO when sulphur emission is present and three filters are available. Many images combine both, for example RGB stars with narrowband nebulosity, or Ha added to the red channel of an RGB galaxy to show its star-forming regions.
